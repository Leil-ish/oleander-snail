Process

Same shape every time. I notice a pattern I can't stop poking at, build the thing that proves it out, then keep the thing honest enough to argue with.

Case study · Decision-support tool

Tradeoff

The open question

Every "which one should I pick" tool ends at price. Real choices don't. People trade money against time, against friction, against how much a thing fits the person they already think they are — and they stop optimizing long before a spreadsheet says they should. I wanted to see what a tool looked like if it took that seriously.

The pattern

The behavioral science already had the parts. Diminishing returns. Identity-based choice. The tax friction charges on every decision. Nobody had wired them together into something you could use. Lined up next to how people actually describe their decisions, the model mostly wrote itself: weight the axes, let the weights differ by person, and let "good enough" count as a finish line instead of a failure.

What it became

Tradeoff scores options across cost, time, friction, identity-fit, and diminishing returns instead of ranking on price. Every weight is visible and editable, because a decision tool you can't argue with is just an opinion wearing a progress bar.

Case study · Live tool

Fair Pay Calculator

The open question

Group practices argue about pay in percentages — 60/40, 70/30 — like the percentage is the fair part. It never was. Once the practice covers what it actually costs to keep a clinician in the room, who gets the margin that's left? That was the number nobody was calculating.

The pattern

Fairness starts after overhead. A split that looks generous on gross goes thin once you subtract rent, software, supervision, payroll; a split that looks stingy can be fine once you see how little surplus the structure throws off. And the math moves with volume — fixed costs spread across more sessions, so a full caseload is genuinely cheaper to carry than a ramping one. An honest tool had to price that tension in instead of pretending one percentage fits every clinician.

What it became

It estimates collected revenue per session, turns monthly overhead into a cost per completed session, and benchmarks the clinician's share against the margin left after overhead. It starts from a different baseline for W-2 and 1099 work, and it raises the bar as the structure produces more surplus. It's a working tool, sitting here as a demonstration. Run your own numbers.

Educational estimate. It's here to show the method, not to replace legal, accounting, or contract review.

What it demonstrates

The same move as Tradeoff. Locate the real unit — here, margin after overhead — and build around it instead of the number everyone reaches for first.
