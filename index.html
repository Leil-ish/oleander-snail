Process

Each piece of work starts as a question I couldn't stop poking at. Same shape every time: notice a pattern, build the thing that proves it, keep the thing honest.

Case study · Decision-support tool

Tradeoff

The open question

Every "which should I pick" tool collapses the decision to price. Real choices don't work that way. People trade money against time, against friction, against how much a thing fits the person they think they are — and they stop optimizing long before the spreadsheet says they should. What would a tool look like if it took that seriously?

The pattern

The behavioral literature already has the pieces — diminishing returns, identity-based choice, the cost of friction. Nobody had wired them into a thing you could actually use. Once I lined those up next to how people describe real decisions, the model wrote itself: weight the axes, let the weights differ per person, and let "good enough" be a legitimate stopping point instead of a failure.

What it became

Tradeoff is a small decision-support tool that scores options across cost, time, friction, identity-fit, and diminishing returns instead of ranking on price alone. It's built to expose its own assumptions — you can see and change every weight — because a decision tool you can't argue with is just an opinion with a progress bar.

Case study · Live tool

Fair Pay Calculator

The open question

Group practices argue about pay in percentages — 60/40, 70/30 — as if the percentage were the fair thing. The percentage is not the fair thing. Once a practice covers what it actually costs to keep a clinician seeing clients, who gets the margin that's left? That's the question nobody was calculating.

The pattern

Fairness starts after overhead, not before it. A split that looks generous on gross can be thin once you subtract rent, software, supervision, and payroll; a split that looks stingy can be reasonable once you see how little surplus the structure actually produces. And the math moves with volume — fixed costs spread across more sessions, so a full caseload is genuinely cheaper to support than a ramping one. Any honest tool had to price that tension in rather than pretend one percentage fits every clinician.

What it became

The calculator estimates collected revenue per session, converts monthly overhead into a cost per completed session, and benchmarks the clinician's share against the post-overhead margin. It starts from a different baseline for W-2 versus 1099 arrangements, and it raises the fairness bar as the structure throws off more surplus. It's a working tool, embedded here as a demonstration piece — run your own numbers.

Educational estimate. It's here to show the method, not to replace legal, accounting, or contract review.

What it demonstrates

The same move as Tradeoff: locate the real unit — here, margin after overhead — and build the tool around it instead of the number everyone argues about.
